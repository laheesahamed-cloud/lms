# Mitral Stenosis — Short Voiceover Script (relaxed pace)

*≈ 360 words. Runs about 2 to 2.5 minutes when read at a calm, unhurried pace. The dashes and commas mark natural breathing points — pause on them. If your text-to-speech tool has a speed slider, set it slightly slower than default for the relaxed feel.*

---

Let's talk about mitral stenosis — and let's take it slow.

So, the mitral valve is the doorway between the left atrium and the left ventricle. Normally, it opens nice and wide — around 4 to 6 square centimetres. In mitral stenosis, that doorway narrows and stiffens, so blood struggles to get through. Below 1 square centimetre, it's severe — though most people don't feel a thing until it's under 2.

Now, why does it happen? More than 9 in 10 cases trace all the way back to rheumatic fever in childhood, usually after an untreated strep throat. So always ask your patient about childhood sore throats or joint pains — that one small question can hand you the whole diagnosis.

So what actually goes wrong? Picture a traffic jam. Blood can't leave the atrium fast enough, so pressure builds up behind the valve. That pressure stretches the atrium — which loves to slip into atrial fibrillation — and it backs up into the lungs, leaving the patient breathless. Given enough time, it wears out the right side of the heart. But here's the classic trap worth holding onto: the left ventricle stays perfectly normal — because the whole problem is sitting just before it.

On examination, look at the face for malar flush — those dusky, plum-red cheeks. Then listen closely, because this valve has a signature sound: a loud first heart sound, a sharp little opening snap, and then a soft, low, rumbling murmur in mid-diastole. And a lovely clue for severity — the closer that snap sits to the second heart sound, the tighter the valve.

To confirm it all, we use an echocardiogram — that's the gold standard.

And how do we treat it? We slow the heart down with a beta-blocker, clear the fluid with a diuretic like furosemide, and if the patient is in atrial fibrillation, we thin the blood for life with warfarin. And please remember this one — the newer blood thinners, the DOACs like rivaroxaban and apixaban, are a hard no in rheumatic mitral stenosis. And if the valve is still soft and flexible, we gently reopen it with a balloon, in a procedure called PTMC.

So that's the whole story — a narrowed valve, rising pressure, a fibrillating atrium. And once you see it as a story, it all just clicks into place.
